Field Notes for New Faculty

No effect sizes on this shelf — these are field notes: small, practical moves collected for new faculty and early-career researchers. Test them the way you would test any intervention: try one for a month, keep what works.
The Wellbeing Shelf  ·  The Faculty Shelf
No. 01 The Calendar

Treat Your Calendar as an Attention Budget, Not a Meeting List

Every week, quietly block one or two whole days — by the day, not by the hour. Treat each as if it were already fully booked with meetings; the meetings are with your research. Colleagues will see a busy calendar and adapt, and your research gets the one resource that never arrives on its own: unbroken time.

What is scarce in academic life today is not information, and not even money; it is attention.

What you protect is what grows.
No. 02 The Morning

Let AI Be Your Morning Cognitive Opener

Give your AI agent a standing brief — your research direction and the methods you are strongest in. Each morning, ask for one random draw: a topic from a field you know nothing about; a question inside your field but outside your area; an advanced method you are weak in; or a fresh paper from a top journal in your field. Ask it to teach you, and to end with a short reading list.

The goal is not immediate utility. It is to put your mind into learning-and-curiosity mode before the day's demands arrive. The effect is strangely real: the mind, like a door, opens from the right key.

Turn AI from an answer machine into a curiosity machine.
No. 03 The First Hour

Your Research Before the Inbox

Before email, before messages, before the news: give the first hour of the working day to your own writing or analysis. Email is other people's agenda for your attention; the early morning is the only part of the day that nobody else has booked yet. Guard it the way you would guard a seminar slot.

An hour of your own work before 9 a.m. is worth three after 5 p.m.
No. 04 The People

A Small Circle Beats a Large Network

Find two or three peers — in or near your field — who will read your drafts, tell you the truth, and expect the same from you. Meet monthly; exchange work before it is ready. Isolation is the hidden tax of early-career research, and it appears on no workload form.

Honest readers are the rarest resource in academia — rarer than funding.
