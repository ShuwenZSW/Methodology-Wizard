The Evidence Shelf

Research runs on the researcher. This shelf collects remedies for stress and anxiety that have real trial evidence behind them — each card states the dose, the strength of the evidence, and the citation. No gurus, no miracle supplements: if it is on this shelf, someone ran the study.
The Wellbeing Shelf  ·  The Evidence Shelf
A note of care. This shelf is educational, not medical advice — it complements professional care; it never replaces it. If anxiety persists for more than two weeks, impairs your work or sleep, or comes with panic attacks or thoughts of self-harm, please contact a licensed mental-health professional or your institution's counseling service. Asking for help early is what the evidence would prescribe.
i.

The Body

sleep · sweat · breath
strong

Aerobic Exercise

30–45 min · 3–5 × / week · brisk

The single best-evidenced remedy on this shelf. Walking fast counts. In the largest observational study of its kind (1.2 million US adults), all exercise types were associated with better mental health, with 30–45 minutes, 3–5 times a week, hitting the optimum.

The evidence Chekroud, S. R. et al. (2018). Association between physical exercise and mental health in 1.2 million individuals in the USA between 2011 and 2015: a cross-sectional study. The Lancet Psychiatry, 5(9), 739–746.
strong

Sleep, Defended

7–9 h · fixed wake time · screens out of the bedroom

The cheapest effect size in the literature. A meta-analysis of randomized trials found that improving sleep quality produced significant, medium-sized improvements in mental health — with larger effects on anxiety and depression than most interventions achieve for insomnia's comorbidities.

The evidence Scott, A. J., Webb, T. L., Martyn-St James, M., Rowse, G., & Weich, S. (2021). Improving sleep quality leads to better mental health: a meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. Sleep Medicine Reviews, 60, 101556.
moderate

Slow, Diaphragmatic Breathing

5–10 min · ~6 breaths/min · belly-led

Inhale ~4 s, exhale ~6 s, let the belly do the work. In an RCT, eight weeks of diaphragmatic breathing significantly lowered salivary cortisol and improved attention and affect. Physiological reviews converge on ~6 breaths per minute as the resonant zone.

The evidence Ma, X. et al. (2017). The effect of diaphragmatic breathing on attention, negative affect and stress in healthy adults. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 11, 432 · Zaccaro, A. et al. (2018). How breath-control can change your life: a systematic review on psycho-physiological correlates of slow breathing. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 12, 353.
moderate

Progressive Muscle Relaxation

10–15 min · head to toe

Tense each muscle group for ~5 seconds, release for ~30, notice the contrast. Meta-analytic evidence shows PMR reliably outperforms waitlist and placebo controls for anxiety — a structured, learnable skill, no equipment.

The evidence Conrad, A., & Roth, W. T. (2007). Muscle relaxation therapy for anxiety disorders: it works but how? Journal of Anxiety Disorders, 21(3), 243–264.
ii.

The Mind

attention training
strong

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

8–12 sessions · guided or structured self-help

The reference standard for anxiety. Meta-analyses of placebo-controlled trials find medium-to-large effects for adult anxiety disorders, and internet-delivered CBT (iCBT) achieves outcomes comparable to face-to-face therapy in controlled comparisons — accessible from any desk.

The evidence Hofmann, S. G., & Smits, J. A. J. (2008). Cognitive-behavioral therapy for adult anxiety disorders: a meta-analysis of randomized placebo-controlled trials. Journal of Clinical Psychiatry, 69(4), 621–632 · Andrews, G. et al. (2018). Computer therapy for the anxiety and depression disorders is effective, acceptable and practical health care: an updated meta-analysis. Journal of Anxiety Disorders, 55, 1–15.
moderate

Mindfulness Meditation (MBSR-style)

8-week course · or 10–20 min/day

Body-scan, breath attention, letting thoughts pass. In a JAMA review of 47 trials with 3,515 participants, mindfulness programs showed moderate evidence for improving anxiety at eight weeks — small-to-moderate effects, but consistently safe, with no evidence of harm.

The evidence Goyal, M. et al. (2014). Meditation programs for psychological stress and well-being: a systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA Internal Medicine, 174(3), 357–368.
moderate

Expressive Writing

15–20 min · 3–4 sessions · private

Write, in an uncensored way, your deepest thoughts and feelings about what is stressing you. Across 146 studies the benefits are small but reliable, and strongest for physical health — one of the few remedies you can do at your desk with a notebook.

The evidence Frattaroli, J. (2006). Experimental disclosure and its moderators: a meta-analysis. Psychological Bulletin, 132(6), 823–865 · Pennebaker, J. W., & Beall, S. K. (1986). Confronting a traumatic event: toward an understanding of inhibition and disease. Journal of Abnormal Psychology, 95(3), 274–281.
iii.

The World

people & nature
strong

Real Social Connection

one real conversation a day · scheduled like a meeting

Across 148 studies (308,849 participants), people with strong social relationships had a 50% greater likelihood of survival — an effect comparable to quitting smoking, and larger than obesity or alcohol. A call beats a scroll: aim for voice or presence, not feeds.

The evidence Holt-Lunstad, J., Smith, T. B., & Layton, J. B. (2010). Social relationships and mortality risk: a meta-analytic review. PLoS Medicine, 7(7), e1000316.
moderate

Nature Exposure / Forest Bathing

≥ 2 h/week · slow walking · senses open

Shinrin-yoku — walk slowly in green space, no phone, attention on senses. In a 20,000-person study, ≥120 minutes per week in nature was the threshold associated with good health and high wellbeing; controlled studies show cortisol and blood-pressure reductions.

The evidence White, M. P. et al. (2019). Spending at least 120 minutes a week in nature is associated with good health and wellbeing. Scientific Reports, 9, 7730 · Hansen, M. M., Jones, R., & Tocchini, K. (2017). Shinrin-Yoku (forest bathing) and nature therapy: a state-of-the-art review. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 14(8), 851.
iv.

The Work

recover on purpose
moderate

Psychological Detachment — the Shutdown Ritual

end-of-day · 5 min · then no email

Built for researchers. The recovery literature finds that mentally switching off from work during off-hours is the single strongest recovery experience predicting wellbeing and lower exhaustion. Concrete protocol: write tomorrow's first step on a card, close the laptop, and let the unfinished be unfinished until morning.

The evidence Sonnentag, S., & Fritz, C. (2007). The Recovery Experience Questionnaire: development and validation of a measure for assessing recuperation and unwinding from work. Journal of Occupational Health Psychology, 12(3), 204–221.
emerging

Micro-breaks, Taken Seriously

5–10 min between deep-work blocks · away from the screen

Short detachment breaks across the workday — standing, stretching, a window, a corridor walk — show accumulating evidence for sustaining attention and lowering end-of-day fatigue. The evidence base is younger than the classics above, but the cost of the experiment is zero.

The evidence Albulescu, P., Macsinga, I., Rusu, A., Sulea, C., Bodnaru, A., & Tulbure, B. T. (2022). "Give me a break!" A systematic review and meta-analysis on the efficacy of micro-breaks for increasing well-being and performance. PLoS ONE, 17(8), e0272460.
