Tiny Atlas · Field Guide No. 6 · Regional & Interlocal Governance

A Field Guide
to Regional Governance

Space is the network's address. Who provides what, where, and at which scale — from Tiebout's foot-voters and Ostrom's polycentric orders to the city-regions where the action moved. The eternal question of local government, with Guide No. 4's networks riding inside it.
Orientation

Foundation

Nine ideas that make "the region" a research object rather than a map drawer — the theoretical stakes of every boundary line and service contract you will ever draw.

Polycentricity

Many autonomous decision centers, overlapping jurisdictions, mutual adjustment under general rules — ordered autonomy, not fragmentation (V. Ostrom, Tiebout & Warren, 1961; E. Ostrom, 1990).

The Tiebout Hypothesis

Residents vote with their feet, sorting into jurisdictions that match their tastes; competition disciplines local governments (Tiebout, 1956). The boldest market metaphor in public administration.

Scale & the Scale-Economy Dilemma

Services have minimum efficient scales that rarely coincide with political boundaries: water wants regions, policing wants neighborhoods, libraries want cities. Scale is a variable, not a slogan.

Fragmentation vs Consolidation

The great normative duel: do many small governments waste and duplicate, or discipline and fit? Fifty years of evidence say it depends on what is measured (Stephens & Wikstrom, 2007).

Interlocal Instruments

Contracts, joint powers agreements, special districts, authorities, cost-sharing — the toolbox through which fragmented jurisdictions act like one system (the ICA lens of Guide No. 4).

Collaborative Regimes

Single agreements transact; regimes endure — stable expectations, shared institutions, repeated interaction across a region's issues (Stephens & Wikstrom, 2007).

The Boundary Problem

Functional regions (watersheds, commuting sheds, labor markets) rarely match any political map. Every regional claim smuggles in a boundary specification — make it explicit.

Multilevel Governance

Authority is layered — supranational, national, regional, local — in Type I (nested, general-purpose) and Type II (overlapping, task-specific) forms (Hooghe & Marks, 2003).

The Rescaled State

Power has drifted to city-regions and metropolitan areas while formal borders lag behind — rescaling as the political geography of late modern governance (Brenner, 2004).

Research Programs

Six Traditions

The field's flagship research programs — from Bloomington's workshop to the theorists of the rescaling state. Read these before drawing your study boundary.

The Workshop School

Ostrom, Tiebout & Warren (1961) · E. Ostrom (1990) · McGinnis (2011)
Polycentric orders can outperform both monopoly and anarchy: autonomous centers compete, learn, and cooperate under shared rules. Design principles for durable self-governance, from irrigation systems to metropolitan police.
Use when explaining how multiple centers sustain cooperation without a boss.

Public Choice & Tiebout

Tiebout (1956) · Brennan & Buchanan (1980)
Jurisdictional competition as discipline: sorting reveals preferences, exit constrains Leviathan. The sternest theoretical case for more governments — and the analytical engine of fragmentation research.
Use when predicting how interjurisdictional competition shapes policy.

Metropolitan Reform

Consolidationists, then Savitch & Vogel (2000) · Stephens & Wikstrom (2007)
One region, one government: capture scale economies, internalize spillovers, equalize capacity. The "new regionalism" answered: cooperation can do what merger cannot — the reform school learned to prefer regimes to mergers.
Use when evaluating consolidation proposals or regional government designs.

Interlocal Management

Feiock (2013) · Andrews & Entwistle (2010)
The supply side of regionalism: which services cities produce, purchase, or share — and how collaboration efficiency varies with risk, trust, and transaction costs. The bridge back to Guide No. 4's networks.
Use when explaining the choice among self-provision, contract, and cooperation.

Multilevel Governance

Hooghe & Marks (2003) · Marks et al. (2008)
Authority is dispersed across nested general-purpose jurisdictions (Type I) and overlapping task-specific regimes (Type II). Regional governance is one layer in a system — Europe's gift to the vocabulary.
Use when the regional question crosses national or supranational layers.

The City-Region School

Brenner (2004) · Scott & Storper (2003)
Globalization rescales governance to city-regions: the economically functional territory is now the metropolitan area, and political authority is being remade to follow — unevenly, politically, and often undemocratically.
Use when the region itself is the moving target of your research.
Inferential Layer

The Model Path

Six steps from "who provides what where?" to defensible claims about regional design. Steps 04–05 are where this guide crosses trails with No. 4 (Policy Networks) and No. 2 (Causes) — regional structure is network structure with a map underneath.

Delineate the Functional Region

Define the region by the problem, not the census: commuting sheds, watersheds, service markets, labor sheds — from flow data, GIS, and stakeholder maps. The boundary is a modeling assumption; state it like one.
Data: flows & geography Entry: none–low

Map the Institutional Landscape

Inventory the supply side: municipalities, counties, special districts, authorities, school and utility boundaries — how many governments touch this region, with what overlaps? Census of Governments data makes this countable.
Data: jurisdictional inventories Entry: low

Measure Structure

Fragmentation indices, Herfindahl concentration of service provision, overlap counts, Tiebout-style choice sets. Structure becomes a variable you can regress — and argue about with numbers instead of adjectives.
Data: counts & shares Entry: low–medium
Framed in the methodology map: Machine Learning Prediction → methodology map.

Explain Interlocal Choice

Why do some cities contract out, some self-provide, some band together? Apply the ICA framework (Guide No. 4): collaboration gains against transaction and distribution costs, with risk and institutions shifting the calculus — then model the ties with ERGM on interlocal agreement networks.
Data: service arrangements + networks Entry: medium–high
Framed in the methodology map: ERGM → methodology map. Framed in the PA map: Collaborative Governance → PA map.

Evaluate Performance

Does fragmentation hurt? Efficiency, equity, accountability, resilience — across structures and cases. Comparative designs shine here: matched jurisdictions, fsQCA for equifinality at medium-n, and longitudinal difference-in-differences around consolidations and dissolutions.
Data: outcomes across structures Entry: medium–high
Framed in the methodology map: QCA (csQCA / fsQCA) → methodology map.

Design & Simulate Reform

The frontier: simulate consolidation, cooperation, or district re-drawing before anyone votes on it — agent-based models with heterogeneous preferences, scale economies, and exit options. The Polycentricity Lab below is the one-variable version of this whole research program.

Supplies

Toolkit & Canon

What we install, and what we keep within arm's reach.

Software

  • GIS & flows: QGIS or R sf/terra for functional regions; commuting and migration matrices from census microdata.
  • Structure indices: R or Python for fragmentation and concentration measures; the Census of Governments and COG-style inventories as data spine.
  • Networks (from Guide No. 4): statnet/ergm for interlocal agreement networks; RSiena when arrangements evolve.
  • Simulation: NetLogo, Mesa (Python), or R for agent-based models of jurisdictional choice — the honest way to argue about reform.
