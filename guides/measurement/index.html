A Field Guide
to Measurement

Before any model, any network, any causal claim — someone decided what to count, how to count it, and whom to ask. From Stevens's scales of measurement to the attenuation formula, this is the craft the other six guides stand on. Base camp for every expedition in this atlas.
Orientation

Foundation

Nine ideas that turn "we measured it" from a slogan into an argument — the difference between data and evidence is every one of these.

Constructs & Operationalization

Trust, performance, fragmentation are concepts, not numbers. Measurement is the explicit bridge from concept to indicator — every coefficient in this atlas crosses that bridge twice.

Scale Levels

Nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio — Stevens (1946) drew the lines that still decide which statistics are legal. Means of Likert items are a convention, not a theorem.

Validity

Does the instrument measure what it claims? Content, construct, criterion — and the MTMM discipline: convergent and discriminant at once, or not at all (Campbell & Fiske, 1959).

Reliability

Would the measure say the same thing twice? Test–retest, parallel forms, internal consistency, inter-rater — consistency is the floor, never the ceiling, of measurement quality.

Classical Test Theory

Observed = true + error (X = T + E). Random error is the friendly enemy: it averages out, but it attenuates every correlation on its way through (Spearman, 1904).

Error vs Bias

Random error blurs; systematic bias distorts. A blurry instrument weakens effects; a biased one invents them. Bias does not wash out with more respondents — it gets more confident.

Sampling & Coverage

The measured are never the target: frames miss people, nonresponse is not random, and coverage error is a measurement problem wearing a sampling costume.

Measurement Invariance

Comparing means across countries or waves assumes the instrument measures the same thing in each. Configural, metric, scalar — a ladder you must climb before you compare.

Triangulation

Multiple imperfect measures beat one confident one — unobtrusive measures, traces, archives, and questions from different angles (Webb et al., 1966).

Research Programs

Six Traditions

The craft's flagship lineages — from Spearman's factor analysis to computational annotation. Read these before you write item 1.

The Psychometric Tradition

Spearman (1904) · Thurstone (1935) · Cronbach (1951) · Lord & Novick (1968)
Latent traits cause responses: factor analysis, item response theory, and the reliability coefficient grew from one insight — that a matrix of inter-item correlations hides a simpler structure. The mathematics of "did we measure the same thing twice?"
Use when you build or borrow multi-item scales and must defend their precision.

The Scaling Tradition

Likert (1932) · Guttman (1944) · Mokken (1971) · Bollen & Lennox (1991)
Turning opinions and events into numbers on a line: summated ratings, cumulative scales, and the reflective–formative distinction that quietly decides whether Cronbach's α is even meaningful. The tradition behind every governance index in Guide No. 6's territory.
Use when your "variable" is a composite of indicators — build it deliberately, not by default.

The Survey Methodology Tradition

Likert (1932) · Groves et al. (2009) · Fowler (2014) · Krosnick & Presser (2010)
The total survey error lens: representation (coverage, sampling, nonresponse) and measurement (question wording, mode, interviewer, respondent cognition) as one system. Asking is a designed instrument, not a conversation.
Use when people must answer your question — design the instrument before you field it.

Registers & Administrative Data

Nordic register tradition · Census of Governments · quality-of-government datasets
States already measure: registers, budgets, personnel files, service records. Cheap, complete, longitudinal — but recorded for someone else's purpose, with concept drift (what "unemployment" means) as the silent tax.
Use when the phenomenon passes through an institution that writes things down.

The Unobtrusive & Field Tradition

Webb, Campbell, Schwartz & Sechrest (1966) · Becker (1958) · Lofland (1971)
Measure without asking: erosion, traces, archives, observation, running records. The antidote to self-report — and the ancestor of today's digital-trace debates. Erosion doesn't answer back; it also doesn't tell you what it meant.
Use when asking changes the answer, or the question cannot be asked at all.

The Computational Tradition

Salganik (2018) · NLP annotation · Guide No. 5's LLM frontier
Digital traces at scale, machine-coded text, LLM-assisted annotation — measurement at industrial speed, with validity questions industrial in size too. Found data measures behavior; whether it measures your construct is still your job.
Use when the trace exists before the question — and validate like it doesn't.
Inferential Layer

The Model Path

Six steps from "we want to study trust in government" to measures others can reuse. Every later guide assumes this path was walked — No. 2 (Causes) inherits your error structure, No. 1 and No. 4 inherit your boundary and name-generator choices, No. 3 inherits your within-case measurements.

Define the Construct

Concept analysis before item generation: dimensions, prototypes and borderline cases, the nomological network of neighbors the construct must relate to — and the neighbors it must not. "Trust" written down is already half-measured.
Data: concepts Entry: none

Choose Indicators & Scale Level

Reflective or formative (Bollen & Lennox, 1991): do the indicators cause the construct or reflect it? Then the Stevens question — which statistics will this scale level permit? — answered before the first respondent, not after the first regression.
Data: items Entry: low

Design the Collection

Probability sampling where inference to a population is claimed; modes, incentives, and field periods as design variables; cognitive pretesting so the respondent hears your question, not yours. Coverage and nonresponse belong on the same page as the questionnaire.
Data: people, registers, or traces Entry: low–medium
Framed in the methodology map: Survey Design & Sampling → methodology map · Weighting & Non-Response Adjustment → methodology map.

Estimate Reliability & Validity

Cronbach's α (or ω, its better-behaved cousin), test–retest where the construct is stable, inter-rater statistics where judgment codes — then convergent and discriminant evidence in the MTMM spirit. Reliability first: attenuation quietly rescales every downstream estimate.
Data: pilot responses Entry: medium
Framed in the methodology map: Psychometrics & Scale Validation → methodology map · IRT & Factor Analysis → methodology map.

Establish Invariance, Then Compare

Configural → metric → scalar, before any cross-group or cross-time mean comparison. When full invariance fails, partial invariance or alignment rescue some comparisons — and when it doesn't, the honest finding is the finding.
Data: multi-group responses Entry: medium–high
Framed in the methodology map: Structural Equation Modelling → methodology map.

Document & Reuse

Codebooks, item wording, translation decisions, field protocols, preregistered measurement plans — measurement is where replication lives or dies. A scale nobody else can reuse is a private language; this atlas is written against private languages.
Data: everything above, written down Entry: none–forever

Supplies

Toolkit & Canon

What we install, and what we keep within arm's reach.

Software & Data

  • Scale construction: R psych and lavaan (CFA, invariance), mirt for IRT; Stata for survey-weighted descriptives.
  • Survey fielding: Qualtrics / LimeSurvey; cognitive pretesting before launch, paradata during.
  • Inter-rater & text: Krippendorff's α, Cohen's κ — and Guide No. 5's frontier when machines join the coding team.
  • Public benchmarks: ANES, GSS, World Values Survey for instruments; ICPSR and IPUMS for reuse; the Quality of Government datasets for validated PA measures.
