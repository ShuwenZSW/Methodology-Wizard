A Field Guide
to Networks

Social Network Analysis, the way the ReGovNet group teaches it to itself: concepts first, measures second, models when the question demands it — with a playground, a toolkit, and the canon.
Orientation

Foundation

Nine ideas everything else in this guide is built from. If a term elsewhere feels fuzzy, come back here.

Nodes & Ties

Actors (people, organizations, governments) and the relationships between them. Ties may be directed or undirected, dichotomous or valued — the data type decides most of what you can do later.

Boundary Specification

Who is in the network is a theoretical decision, not a technical one (Laumann et al., 1983). Every network finding is conditional on its boundary — state it.

One-mode vs Two-mode

Actor-by-actor matrices versus actor-by-event incidence matrices. Projecting two-mode data (e.g., boards → interlocks) inflates ties; say so when you do it.

Density

Realized ties ÷ possible ties. Highly sensitive to network size and boundary — compare densities only within comparably bounded networks.

Reciprocity & Transitivity

The two local rules that generate structure: ties tend to be returned, and friends of friends tend to become friends. The raw material of balance and clustering theory.

Triads

Sixteen possible directed triad types (Holland & Leinhardt, 1976); each is a structural signature — hierarchy, brokerage, or closure.

Homophily

Similarity breeds connection (McPherson, Smith-Lovin & Cook, 2001). The perennial confound: do similar nodes connect, or do connected nodes converge?

Structural Holes

Actors bridging disconnected groups occupy structural holes (Burt, 1992) — brokerage advantage, measured by constraint.

Small Worlds

High local clustering with surprisingly short global paths (Watts & Strogatz, 1998) — the architecture of fast diffusion and short reach.

Descriptive Layer

Measures

Centrality answers "who occupies which position?" — but each family of measures answers a different who. Choose the measure that matches your theory of what flows through the network.

Degree Centrality

Shaw (1954) · Freeman (1979)
The number of ties a node has. Direct, local, robust — the simplest count of embeddedness or raw activity.
Use when prominence within the immediate neighborhood matters: visibility, direct exposure, communication load.

Betweenness Centrality

Freeman (1977)
The share of all shortest paths that pass through a node. Captures brokerage and gatekeeping — being between groups rather than merely active.
Use when a node can control diffusion, information, or coordination — the structural power measure.

Closeness Centrality

Bavelas (1950) · Sabidussi (1966)
Reciprocal of average distance to all other nodes. How efficiently a node can reach everyone — independence from mediators.
Use when speed of access or reach matters and the network has no giant disconnected component.

Eigenvector & PageRank

Bonacich (1972) · Page et al. (1999)
Centrality weighted by the centrality of one's alters: it is not how many you know, but who you know.
Use when prestige, status diffusion, or influence-through-connection is the mechanism.
At the network level, pair these with centralization (how unequal the distribution is), clustering coefficient (local closure), and average path length. See how the map frames the centrality family: Centrality Measures → methodology map.
Inferential Layer

The Model Path

Five models in the order we would teach them. Each step answers a harder question and demands more of your data. Stop where your question stops.

QAP / MRQAP

Regress one network on other networks or covariate matrices, testing coefficients by permutation of rows and columns. The permutation respects the autocorrelation that ordinary regression ignores, making standard errors defensible.
Data: one-mode matrices, 1 wave Entry: low
Framed in the methodology map: Social Network Analysis → methodology map.

Community Detection & Blockmodels

Find cohesive subgroups or equivalent positions: modularity maximization (Louvain), spectral methods, or classic CONCOR blockmodeling. Exploratory — generates hypotheses about where the seams of a network are.
Data: any cross-section Entry: low

ERGM — Exponential Random Graph Models

Model tie formation: which local configurations — edges, reciprocity, triangles, homophily, popularity — generate the observed network? Each parameter is a structural tendency, tested while holding the others constant. The workhorse of cross-sectional network statistics.
Data: complete network, 1 wave Entry: medium
Framed in the methodology map: ERGM → methodology map.

Latent Space Models & Stochastic Blockmodels

Place nodes in a latent geometry (Hoff, Raftery & Handcock, 2002) or assign them to unobserved blocks (Nowicki & Snijders, 2001); ties become more likely at short latent distance or within the same block. Goodness-of-fit visualization comes naturally.
Data: complete network, 1 wave Entry: medium

SAOM / SIENA — Stochastic Actor-Oriented Models

The model of network dynamics (Snijders, 2001; Snijders, van de Bunt & Steglich, 2010): actors make tie choices given their local network, micro-steps accumulate into panel change — and, jointly, behavior and network co-evolve. This is the design that can finally separate selection from influence.
Data: panel networks, 2+ waves Entry: high

Supplies

Toolkit & Canon

What we install, and what we keep within arm's reach.

Software

  • R: statnet (ergm, network, sna), igraph, RSiena, tidygraph + ggraph for grammar-of-graphics plotting.
  • Python: networkx, graph-tool, python-igraph — for pipelines and large graphs.
  • GUI: Gephi (exploration), UCINET + NetDraw (classics), Pajek (large networks), visone, E-NET (egonets), ORA, NodeXL (Excel-native).
  • Datasets: Add Health, Stanford SNAP, KONECT, Netzschleuder, plus the datasets bundled with UCINET and RSiena.

Canon

  • Wasserman & Faust (1994), Social Network Analysis: Methods and Applications — the bible.
  • Scott (2017, 4th ed.), Social Network Analysis — the short on-ramp.
  • Borgatti, Everett & Johnson (2013), Analyzing Social Networks — the practical handbook.
  • Newman (2010), Networks: An Introduction — the mathematical foundations.
  • Easley & Kleinberg (2010), Networks, Crowds, and Markets — the CS–economics crossover.
  • Robins (2015), Doing Social Network Research — research design and boundaries.
  • Snijders, van de Bunt & Steglich (2010) — the SAOM/SIENA paper in Social Networks 32(1).
  • McPherson, Smith-Lovin & Cook (2001) — "Birds of a Feather," Annual Review of Sociology.
