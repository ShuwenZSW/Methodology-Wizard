Tiny Atlas · Field Guide No. 7 · Policy Diffusion

A Field Guide
to Policy Diffusion

Why governments copy each other. From Walker's regional waves and Berry & Berry's event histories to the four-mechanism framework — learning, imitation, competition, coercion — the exposure matrix, Galton's problem, and the frontier of text-based policy similarity. Where Guide No. 1's networks become influence and No. 2's identification becomes survival analysis.
Orientation

Foundation

Nine ideas that turn "everyone seems to be doing it" into a research design — the conceptual stakes of every adjacency matrix and hazard model you will ever fit.

Diffusion

Interdependence: one government's policy choices systematically influence another's — through observation, competition, or hierarchy (Berry & Berry, 1990). The unit of analysis quietly becomes the dyad.

Convergence & Galton's Problem

Similar policies from similar conditions — wealth, ideology, shocks — without any influence. The look-alike problem (Heichel, Pape & Sommerer, 2005): correlation across governments is not proof of contagion.

Learning

Updating beliefs about a policy's consequences from others' experience — rational but bounded (Meseguer, 2005); strongest when others' success is observable and transferable (Volden, 2006).

Imitation & Social Influence

Adopting for legitimacy, identity, or simple availability — "they did it, and they are like us" (Strang & Meyer, 1993; Karch, 2007). The mechanism of emulation, not evaluation.

Competition

Strategic interdependence through mobile capital and yardstick comparisons — tax cuts chase tax cuts, standards chase standards; races to the bottom, or occasionally the top.

Coercion

Hierarchy does the spreading: federal mandates, grant conditions, international pressure. Vertical by definition — obedience, not observation (Shipan & Volden, 2008).

Horizontal & Vertical

Among equals — states, cities, nations — or up and down a hierarchy, including bottom-up: cities dragging their states forward (Shipan & Volden, 2006).

The Exposure Matrix W

Who is exposed to whom: borders, distance, trade, IGOs, professional ties, shared parties. W is a theory — every entry is a claim about a channel (LeSage & Pace, 2009).

Thresholds & Cascades

Adopt when enough neighbors have adopted. Granovetter's (1978) thresholds and Watts's (2002) global cascades explain why the same policy erupts in one decade and stalls in another.

Research Programs

Five Traditions

The field's flagship research programs — from the first adoption-wave maps to mechanism tests and cross-national spatial econometrics. Read in this order and the literature reads itself.

Waves & Regions

Walker (1969) · Gray (1973) · Mooney (2001)
The founding empirical program: map when each American state adopted an innovation and test whether neighbors adopt together. Walker pitted "internal determinants" against "national interaction"; Gray added regional leader–follower clusters; Mooney modeled regional effects explicitly. The S-curve and the map are born here.
Use when describing adoption timing and testing whether geography clusters it.

Event-History Diffusion

Berry & Berry (1990, 1992) · Karch (2007)
The methodological charter: model adoption as a hazard — the rate at which not-yet-adopters succumb, as a function of their own traits and their neighbors' adoptions. Lotteries, income taxes, and a hundred policies since have been run through the hazard lens.
Use when you have adoption dates and want a defensible estimate of neighbor influence.

The Mechanisms Program

Shipan & Volden (2006, 2008) · Boehmke & Witmer (2004) · Volden (2006)
Diffusion is not one thing: learning, imitation, competition, coercion travel through different channels — horizontal and vertical — and predict different signatures. Success emulation, policy attributes, and dyadic sources turn "is there diffusion?" into "which mechanism?"
Use when the interesting claim is about how influence travels, not merely whether.

Policy Attributes & Complexity

Boushey (2010) · Makse & Volden (2011) · Nicholson-Crotty (2009)
Not every policy diffuses the same way: complexity, salience, observability, cost set the speed and shape of the cascade. Complex policies creep through professional networks; simple symbolic ones race along the evening news.
Use when comparing across policies or explaining why one policy outran another.

The Global & Comparative Turn

Simmons & Elkins (2004) · Braun & Gilardi (2006) · Neumayer & Plümper (2010)
Nations learn from nations: liberalization waves, IGOs as classrooms, and the econometrics of spatially dependent dyadic data. Galton's problem becomes a full identification agenda — common shocks, correlated errors, endogenous networks — with robustness tests to match.
Use when the adopters are countries and the channels are global.
Inferential Layer

The Model Path

Six steps from "everyone seems to be doing it" to defensible claims about influence. Steps 04–06 are where this guide crosses trails with No. 1 (Networks) and No. 2 (Causes) — the exposure matrix is a network, and the hazard model is an identification design wearing a clock.

Delimit & Date the Policy

Define what counts as adoption — the law, the program, the amendment — and collect the dates. The frontier does this with text similarity over legal corpora instead of hand-coding, but the classic standard is a defended coding rule and a citation for every date.
Data: statutes & codes Entry: none–low

Draw the S-Curve

Cumulative adoption over time: laggards, take-off, saturation (Rogers, 2003). Compare against a baseline hazard — what adoption would look like without interdependence. The gap between the curves is the phenomenon; the map of who sits inside it is the puzzle.
Data: adoption dates Entry: none

Name the Mechanism

Learning, imitation, competition, coercion — crossed with horizontal and vertical channels (Shipan & Volden, 2008). Each mechanism implies different exposure: learners watch the successful, imitators watch the similar, competitors watch the rival, the coerced obey the superior. The mechanism chooses your W.
Data: theory Entry: low
Framed in the PA map: Policy Diffusion → PA map.

Build the Exposure Matrix

W encodes the claimed channels: contiguity and distance (geography), trade, IGO memberships, party ties, professional associations (networks), and hierarchy (vertical). Row-standardize or not — deliberately. Every W is a falsifiable statement about who watches whom.
Data: geography + network ties Entry: medium
Framed in the methodology map: ERGM → methodology map. Framed in Guide No. 4: Policy Networks →.

Estimate the Hazard

Event-history models with time-varying exposure: the clock ticks, each not-yet-adopter faces a hazard that rises with neighbors' adoptions (Berry & Berry, 1990). For continuous policies, turn to spatial lag / Durbin models and network-autocorrelation diagnostics — and remember the hazard's hidden assumption: conditional independence of the clock.
Data: panel of adopters Entry: medium–high
Framed in the methodology map: Survival & Event History Analysis → methodology map · Geospatial Analysis & GIS → methodology map.

Rule Out the Look-Alikes

The frontier of credibility: Galton's problem. Common shocks (a national crisis), correlated disturbances, and endogenous W all masquerade as diffusion. Discipline them with exogenous variation, placebo exposures, spatial-robust standard errors, and design-based identification — the Cascade Lab below shows why this step is not optional.
Data: a design, not just data Entry: high–frontier
Framed in Guide No. 2: Causes →.

The Cascade Lab

Sixty governments on a small-world network. Each adopts once enough of its peers have — or, in the other world, nobody influences anybody and adoptions are independent draws. Stylized model: ring lattice with random long-distance shortcuts (à la Watts–Strogatz, but the ring is kept intact — shortcuts are added, not substituted); a government adopts when its adopted-neighbor share reaches its threshold. In the common-shock world there is no influence at all — every adoption is an independent draw — yet the raw cross-section can still look "clustered." The neighbor-correlation meter is computed live on the current network; the teaching point is Neumayer & Plümper (2010) and Braun & Gilardi (2006): spatial correlation is a symptom, not a mechanism.
Supplies

Toolkit & Canon

What we install, and what we keep within arm's reach.

Software

  • Event history: R survival/flexsurv or Stata stcox/streg for adoption hazards with time-varying exposure (the Berry & Berry engine).
  • Spatial models: R spdep / spatialreg for W-weighted lags, Moran's I, and spatial Durbin models; sf for the geography underneath.
  • Network channels: statnet/ergm and RSiena (from Guide No. 1) when the exposure matrix itself must be modeled — policy networks as influence channels.
  • Thresholds & cascades: NetLogo or Python Mesa for the agent-based version of the Cascade Lab on empirical networks.
  • Text similarity (frontier): Python scikit-learn TF-IDF + cosine for policy-copy detection across legal corpora.

The Frontier

Where the Field Is Moving

Five live frontiers — from measuring the policy itself with machines to identifying mechanisms with designs. This is the far half of the trail.

Text-as-Data Similarity

Who copied whom, measured: embed statutes and regulations, score mimicry with cosine similarity, and date the copying — policy diffusion detection without hand-coding 50 states (the ML wing of Guide No. 5).

Design-Based Identification

Exogenous shocks to exposure — new borders, dissolved IGOs, federal waivers — as instruments for influence; placebo exposures and spatial-robust inference as routine hygiene (with Guide No. 2).

Empirical Thresholds & Cascades

Fitting Granovetter–Watts models to real adoption networks: heterogeneous thresholds, critical-mass estimation, and predicting which policy will cascade next — the Cascade Lab's serious older sibling.

Mechanism-Specific Networks

Separate W for each mechanism — success-weighted W for learning, similarity-W for imitation, rivalry-W for competition — and let the data arbitrate between them (dyadic sources, attribute interactions).

Global Diffusion & Translation

Ideas travel and mutate: policy transfer, translation, and mobilities across national and sectoral boundaries (Peck & Theodore, 2010; Campbell, 2004) — diffusion research meets sociology's world-society school.

The Meta-Frontier

Diffusion research has itself diffused — into IO, IR, comparative politics, and public health — carrying its methods with it (Graham, Shipan & Volden, 2013). The map of the map keeps redrawing itself.
