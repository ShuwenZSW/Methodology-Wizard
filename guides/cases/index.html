Tiny Atlas · Field Guide No. 3

A Field Guide
to Cases

Case study research and process tracing, the way the ReGovNet group practices it: how a single case can carry causal weight — bounding the case, choosing it deliberately, and reading evidence like a detective.
Orientation

Foundation

Nine ideas everything else in this guide is built from. Small-n research fails rarely from statistics — it fails from sloppy case choices and misread evidence.

The Case as Unit

Bounding the case is the first analytic act (Ragin, 1992): what is the "whole" you claim to study — a city, a reform episode, a network — and where does it end?

Case Selection

Purposive, not random: typical, diverse, extreme, deviant, influential, or most-similar cases each buy a different kind of inference (Seawright & Gerring, 2008). Selecting on the outcome alone invites bias (Geddes, 1990).

Mill's Methods

Most-similar systems (difference-finding) and most-different systems (common-finding) comparisons — the classical logic underneath all comparative design.

CPOs vs DSOs

Causal-process observations shed light on mechanisms inside one case; dataset observations covary across many (Collier, Brady & Seawright, 2010). Research designs trade in both currencies.

Mechanisms

Explanation means opening the black box: the entities and activities that link cause to outcome (Hedström & Ylikoski, 2010). Cases are where mechanisms are visible.

Process Tracing

Reconstruct the sequence of events within a case to test whether the hypothesized mechanism actually left its fingerprints (George & Bennett, 2005; Beach & Pedersen, 2013).

Evidence Typology

Van Evera's (1997) four types — straw-in-the-wind, hoop, smoking gun, doubly decisive — grade evidence by how decisively it discriminates between hypotheses. The basis of the Evidence Lab below.

Necessity & Sufficiency

Set-theoretic logic: a condition may be necessary (outcome can't occur without it), sufficient (its presence always triggers it), or part of a recipe — the grammar of QCA (Ragin, 1987).

Equifinality & Scope

Different causal recipes can reach the same outcome, and mechanisms travel only within scope conditions. Claim both the paths and the boundaries of your explanation.

Design Layer

Case Designs

Six ways to organize one or a few cases so they can answer a big question. Each buys a different inference at a different price.

The Critical Case

Eckstein (1975) · Flyvbjerg (2006)
Choose a case where theory makes its most demanding prediction — if the theory survives here, it earns confidence everywhere; if it fails here, it fails hard.
Use when a theory is mature enough to risk, and one decisive arena exists.

Comparative Cases (Mill)

Mill (1843) · Przeworski & Teune (1970)
Most-similar: match on everything except the suspect variable. Most-different: find wildly different cases with one common outcome — what do they share?
Use when 2–8 cases exist and background knowledge supports controlled comparison.

Process-Tracing Case

George & Bennett (2005) · Bennett & Checkel (2015)
Stay inside one case and test whether the causal chain left the observable implications only that mechanism could produce — predictions competitors cannot match.
Use when one case matters and rich within-case material (documents, interviews, timelines) exists.

Structured-Focused Comparison

George (1979)
Ask the same questions of every case, defined by a shared framework, so comparison becomes disciplined rather than impressionistic.
Use when comparing several cases and worried about drifting questions.

fsQCA — Set-Theoretic Comparison

Ragin (1987, 2008) · Schneider & Wagemann (2012)
Treat conditions and outcomes as sets; find the recipes (sufficient combinations) and core conditions (necessity) that produce an outcome, with consistency and coverage as the quality checks.
Use when 10–50 cases, medium-n, and you suspect multiple conjunctural causation.

Nested & Multi-Method

Lieberman (2005) · Tarrow (2010)
Let a large-n analysis select and motivate cases — model-testing or model-building nested designs — then dig inside the cases with process tracing; each layer disciplines the other.
Use when both a quantitative pattern and case-level material are available.
Inferential Layer

The Model Path

Six steps from a vague interest to a defensible case-based claim. Identification logic first — just like Guide No. 2, in its small-n dialect.

Bound the Case & the Question

Define the case, the outcome, and the rival explanations before touching data. Write the scope conditions and the observable implications of each hypothesis — this is the case-research analogue of the DAG.
Data: theory and curiosity Entry: none

Select Cases Deliberately

Choose by the inferential job: typical, diverse, extreme, deviant, influential, or most-similar. Report the selection rule and its risks — random selection is rarely the right tool at n = 4.
Data: universe of candidate cases Entry: low
Framed in the methodology map: Most Similar / Different Systems → methodology map.

Process Tracing Within the Case

Test the mechanism's fingerprints: sequence analysis, predicting the least-likely observations, and explicit Bayesian updating over pieces of evidence (Bennett, 2010; Fairfield & Charman, 2017). Chain evidence, don't stack anecdotes.
Data: documents, interviews, timelines Entry: medium
Framed in the methodology map: Process Tracing → methodology map.

Compare Across Cases

Structured-focused questions, Mill's methods, or comparative historical analysis: hold context constant, vary the suspect, and watch the outcome — then confront the explanation with the least convenient case.
Data: 2–10 comparable cases Entry: medium
Framed in the methodology map: Comparative Historical Analysis → methodology map.

fsQCA — Recipes at Medium-n

Calibrate conditions into fuzzy sets, build the truth table, minimize to sufficient recipes, and report necessity/consistency/coverage. QCA is comparison formalized — and it preaches equifinality by construction.
Data: ~10–50 cases, calibrated conditions Entry: medium–high
Framed in the methodology map: QCA (csQCA / fsQCA) → methodology map.

Nested Analysis — Marry the Large and the Small

Close the loop with Guide No. 2: use regression to locate anomalies, cases to explain them (Lieberman, 2005); or build theory in cases, then test it at scale. The triangulation is the point.
Data: large-n plus within-case material Entry: high
Framed in the methodology map: QCA + Regression Triangulation → methodology map.

Supplies

Toolkit & Canon

What we install, and what we keep within arm's reach.

Software

  • QCA: R packages SetMethods, QCApro, frscore; the original fsQCA software; QCAGUI for a point-and-click start.
  • Qualitative coding: NVivo, ATLAS.ti, MAXQDA — or the open-source QualCoder; LLM-assisted coding is emerging fast (see the methodology map).
  • Within-case analysis: R/Python for sequence and timeline work; archival databases for historical cases.
  • Benchmark materials: Schneider & Wagemann's replication data for Set-Theoretic Methods; the classic fsQCA democracy studies (Ragin & Pennings).

Canon

  • Yin (2018), Case Study Research and Applications — the design handbook.
  • George & Bennett (2005), Case Studies and Theory Development — the foundational text.
  • Beach & Pedersen (2019), Process-Tracing Methods — the modern manual.
  • Van Evera (1997), Guide to Methods for Students of Political Science — where the evidence typology lives.
  • Bennett & Checkel (2015), Process Tracing: From Metaphor to Analytic Tool.
  • Ragin (1987, 2008), The Comparative Method and Redesigning Social Inquiry — set-theoretic foundations.
  • Schneider & Wagemann (2012), Set-Theoretic Methods for the Social Sciences — the QCA standard.
  • Fairfield & Charman (2017) — explicit Bayesian process tracing, AJPS.
