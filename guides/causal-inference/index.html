Tiny Atlas · Field Guide No. 2

A Field Guide
to Causes

Causal inference, the way the ReGovNet group's methodologists teach it: what would have happened, had things been different — from potential outcomes to DAGs, from natural experiments to causal machine learning.
Orientation

Foundation

Nine ideas everything else in this guide is built from. Causal inference is not a technique — it is a discipline of stating what you must believe before any number can be trusted.

Potential Outcomes

Each unit has two possible outcomes, Y(1) and Y(0) (Rubin, 1974). The causal effect is their difference — and only one of the two is ever observed.

The Counterfactual

The missing potential outcome is the fundamental problem of causal inference. Every design in this guide is a different strategy for approximating it.

Ignorability

Treatment assignment is independent of potential outcomes, given observed covariates X ("selection on observables"). The assumption that good design buys — rarely for free.

Overlap (Positivity)

Every unit needs some chance of either treatment. Where overlap fails, estimates silently extrapolate into regions with no comparable data.

Consistency & SUTVA

The treatment is well-defined, and units do not interfere with one another. Spillovers and network effects violate SUTVA — where Guide No. 1 takes over.

Confounding

A variable that causes both treatment and outcome. The classic bias — its direction is unknowable without theory, which is why design must precede data.

Colliders

A common effect of treatment and outcome. Conditioning on a collider opens a non-causal path and manufactures association — the most dangerous "control variable."

DAGs & the Backdoor

Draw your assumptions before your model (Pearl, 1995). A DAG makes confounding visible; the backdoor criterion says exactly what to adjust for — and what to leave alone.

Identification vs Estimation

Identification is a claim about the world (design); estimation is arithmetic on data. No estimator, however sophisticated, repairs failed identification.

Design Layer

Identification Designs

Six strategies for making the counterfactual credible. Each trades on a different source of as-if randomness — choose the one your setting actually offers.

Randomized Controlled Trials

Fisher (1925) · Neyman (1923/1990)
Randomization makes treatment assignment independent of everything, observed and unobserved — ignorability by construction, balance in expectation.
Use when you can assign treatment; field and survey experiments extend the logic to real-world settings.

Instrumental Variables

Wright (1928) · Angrist, Imbens & Rubin (1996)
A variable that moves treatment but affects the outcome only through it — isolating variation that is as-if random, even with unobserved confounding.
Use when a credible instrument exists; the estimand is a local effect for compliers, not the whole population.

Difference-in-Differences

Card & Krueger (1994)
Compare how a treated group changes relative to an untreated group. Parallel trends substitute for ignorability: confounders may exist, but their effect must be common.
Use when a policy arrives for some units and not others, and pre-trends look alike.

Regression Discontinuity

Thistlethwaite & Campbell (1960)
Units just above and just below an eligibility cutoff are near-identical, so the jump in outcomes at the threshold estimates the effect — a local experiment at the boundary.
Use when treatment follows a score threshold — budgets, test cutoffs, eligibility indices.

Matching & Weighting

Rosenbaum & Rubin (1983)
Construct the missing counterfactual from observables: match or reweight untreated units to resemble treated ones on the propensity score. Balance, not model fit, is the target.
Use when all confounders are measured; report covariate balance, not just estimates.

Synthetic Control

Abadie & Gardeazabal (2003) · Abadie, Diamond & Hainmueller (2010)
Build the counterfactual unit as a weighted combination of untreated units that reproduces the treated unit's pre-period trajectory — then study the divergence.
Use when one unit (city, region, country) is treated and many donors are available.
Inferential Layer

The Model Path

Six steps in the order we would teach them. Identification first, estimation second — stop where your design stops.

DAGs & Backdoor Adjustment

Start here — it costs nothing but honesty. Write down what causes what, find every backdoor path from treatment to outcome, and adjust for a sufficient set of confounders while leaving colliders and mediators untouched.
Data: theory, then covariates Entry: none
Framed in the methodology map: DAGs & Causal Identification → methodology map.

Matching & Weighting

Estimate the propensity score, then match or weight so treated and control groups look alike on observables (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983; Hainmueller, 2012). Diagnose with standardized mean differences and overlap plots — not with the propensity model's fit statistics.
Data: cross-section, rich covariates Entry: low
Framed in the methodology map: Propensity Score Matching → methodology map.

Difference-in-Differences & Event Studies

The workhorse of policy evaluation. Double-robust modern estimators (Callaway & Sant'Anna, 2021; Goodman-Bacon diagnostics) handle staggered adoption, where two-way fixed effects goes wrong. Always show pre-trends.
Data: treated + comparison, 2+ periods Entry: low–medium
Framed in the methodology map: Difference-in-Differences → methodology map.

Regression Discontinuity

Estimate the local effect at a cutoff: continuity of potential outcomes implies the outcome jump at the threshold is causal. Manipulation tests (McCrary density), bandwidth sensitivity, and donut robustness are the credibility package.
Data: running variable + cutoff Entry: medium
Framed in the methodology map: Regression Discontinuity → methodology map.

Synthetic Control

For one treated unit, assemble a synthetic twin from donor weights chosen to fit the pre-period — then the post-period gap is the effect (Abadie, Diamond & Hainmueller, 2010). Augmented SCM and placebo in-time tests carry the inference.
Data: 1 treated unit, many donors, long pre-period Entry: medium
Framed in the methodology map: Synthetic Control Method → methodology map.

Causal Machine Learning

When effects differ across units, estimate heterogeneity directly: double/debiased machine learning removes nuisance variation with flexible learners (Chernozhukov et al., 2018); causal forests estimate conditional average treatment effects with honest inference (Wager & Athey, 2018).
Data: large n, many covariates Entry: high
Framed in the methodology map: Causal Machine Learning → methodology map.
Laboratory

Feel the Confounding

A living simulation. An outcome Y rises with a covariate X — and the probability of treatment T also rises with X. Turn the confounding dial, then watch the naive estimate chase the treatment group upward while the adjusted estimate holds the line at the true effect.
True effect
3.0
Naive estimate (Y ~ T)
–
Adjusted (Y ~ T + X)
–
True model: Y = 2 + 1.8·X + 3·T + noise. Treatment probability rises with X as confounding strengthens. The naive regression of Y on T alone absorbs X's effect into the treatment coefficient; adjusting for X recovers the truth — if X is measured.
Supplies

Toolkit & Canon

What we install, and what we keep within arm's reach.

Software

  • R: fixest and did (Callaway–Sant'Anna) for DiD, rdrobust for discontinuities, MatchIt/WeightIt for balance, Synth/gsynth/augsynth for synthetic control, DoubleML and grf for causal ML.
  • Python: DoWhy and EconML (causal pipelines), linearmodels, CausalML, statsmodels for the classics.
  • Stata: reghdfe, teffects, rdrobust, synth — the policy-evaluation workbench.
  • Benchmark datasets: the NSW/Lalonde (1986) job-training benchmark, Card & Krueger's (1994) fast-food panel, Cattaneo's RD data archive.

Canon

  • Angrist & Pischke (2009), Mostly Harmless Econometrics — the field bible.
  • Angrist & Pischke (2015), Mastering 'Metrics — the gentler on-ramp.
  • Cunningham (2021), Causal Inference: The Mixtape — free online, unusually humane.
  • Morgan & Winship (2015), Counterfactuals and Causal Inference — the DAG perspective.
  • Imbens & Rubin (2015), Causal Inference — potential outcomes, systematically.
  • Pearl (2009), Causality — the foundations; Chapter 1 suffices for most.
  • Rosenbaum (2020), Design of Observational Studies — sensitivity analysis as craft.
  • Callaway & Sant'Anna (2021) and Abadie, Diamond & Hainmueller (2010) — the modern DiD and synthetic-control papers.
