A Field Guide
to Policy Networks

Where Guide No. 1's toolkit meets the Public Administration map's questions: policy networks, governance networks, and interlocal collaboration — how public organizations govern together, and how to study it without fooling yourself.
Orientation

Foundation

Nine ideas that make networks a public administration topic and not just a method — the theoretical stakes of every centrality score you will ever compute.

Policy Networks

Public and private actors bound by resource dependencies, shaping policy through exchange, not hierarchy (Laumann & Knoke, 1987). The state is one actor among many.

Governance Networks

When interdependence persists, networks become the mode of governance itself — negotiated, self-organizing, hard to steer (Klijn & Koppenjan, 2016).

The Hollow State

Implementation through contracts and third parties hollows out direct control: government steers rather than rows (Milward & Provan, 2000). Network structure becomes the delivery system.

Whole Networks vs Ego Networks

Whole networks map a bounded system (a region's emergency managers); ego networks map one actor's neighborhood. Boundary specification (see Guide No. 1) is a governance claim, not a sampling footnote.

Institutional Collective Action

Collaboration beats fragmentation but is expensive: transaction costs and distribution conflicts keep efficient partnerships unformed (Feiock, 2013). The central dilemma of regional governance.

Belief Systems & Structure

Who talks to whom depends on what actors believe — science, risk, and ideology shape policy network structure (Henry, Lubell & McCoy, 2011; Weible et al.).

Brokerage in Governance

Actors bridging disconnected groups control information and resources — structural holes theory applied to administrators, brokers, and boundary spanners (Burt, 1992; Gould & Fernandez).

Network Effectiveness

No structure is best in general: effectiveness depends on contingencies — stability of environment, task complexity, trust (Provan & Milward, 1995). Context is a variable, not an apology.

Network Management

Managers don't command networks — they manage the conditions of interaction: activate actors, facilitate, broker (Kickert, Klijn & Koppenjan, 1997; Agranoff & McGuire, 2001).

Research Programs

Six Traditions

The field's flagship research programs, each pairing a theoretical claim with a network-analytic design. Read these before designing your own.

The Organizational State

Laumann & Knoke (1987) · Knoke et al. (1996)
National policy domains as interorganizational influence networks: reputational influence, information exchange, and coalition behavior in energy and health.
Use when studying national policy domains with reputational and exchange data.

Effectiveness in the Hollow State

Provan & Milward (1995)
Mental health delivery networks in four U.S. communities: centralized integration works under stable conditions; decentralized brokerage when environments shift — the field's most cited contingency claim.
Use when linking structure to performance in contracted service systems.

Self-Organizing Policy Networks

Berardo & Scholz (2010)
Risk drives structure: actors facing collective-risk problems build closure (trust) locally while bridging the whole — ERGM applied to estuary governance networks across the U.S.
Use when explaining why some collaborative networks form and others never do.

Belief Systems & Social Capital

Henry, Lubell & McCoy (2011) · Mewhirter, Berardo & Scholz (2018)
Belief similarity builds ties, and social capital structures cooperation in ecology policy — then extended to polycentric governance systems, where risk drives asymmetric bridging among institutions.
Use when ideology or risk framing plausibly drives collaboration.

Institutional Collective Action

Feiock (2013)
A general framework for local government collaboration: collaboration gains vs transaction and distribution costs, with collaboration risk and existing institutions shifting the calculus.
Use when explaining (non-)cooperation among cities, districts, or agencies.

Collaborative Public Management

Agranoff & McGuire (2003, 2014)
What public managers actually do in networks — activation, framing, mobilizing, synthesizing — measured in economic development networks across U.S. cities and states.
Use when the manager, not the structure, is your explanatory variable.
Inferential Layer

The Model Path

Six steps from "who collaborates with whom?" to causal claims about governance. Steps 04–05 are where this guide crosses trails with No. 1 (Networks) and No. 2 (Causes) — network questions are causal questions wearing a disguise.

Map the Policy Network

Define the boundary (the region, the policy domain, the governance task), choose relations that match the theory (advice, resources, collaboration, beliefs), and collect data: surveys, meeting records, grants, board interlocks, emails.
Data: bounded population of actors Entry: none–low
Framed in the methodology map: Social Network Analysis → methodology map. Framed in the PA map: Governance & Network Theory → PA map.

Describe with a Contingency Lens

Density, centralization, core–periphery, brokerage positions — but always against Provan & Milward's question: effective for what, under which conditions? A dense network is not a score; it is a hypothesis about fit.
Data: one whole network Entry: low

ERGM: Explain the Ties

What generates collaboration — homophily of beliefs and size, popularity, activity, closure under risk (Berardo & Scholz), bridging under complexity? ERGMs test these signatures while accounting for the network's very existence as data.
Data: complete network, 1 wave Entry: medium
Framed in the methodology map: ERGM → methodology map.

Dynamics: SAOM / SIENA

Networks co-evolve with beliefs and performance: does trust create ties, or do ties create trust? Stochastic actor-oriented models on panel network data separate selection from influence — the network version of Guide No. 2's hardest confound.
Data: panel networks, 2+ waves Entry: high

Causal Claims: Spillovers & Peer Effects

When outcomes travel along ties, SUTVA is violated (see Guide No. 2) — neighbors are not independent observations. Use network autocorrelation models, spatial designs over the network, or design-based identification of peer effects (randomized interventions at the node level).
Data: network + outcome + design Entry: high

Network Performance & Design

The frontier: linking structure to outcomes across many networks (multilevel ERGM, meta-analyses of network studies), and prescriptive work — how should a regional governance system be rewired to perform?
Data: many networks + outcomes Entry: frontier

Supplies

Toolkit & Canon

What we install, and what we keep within arm's reach.

Software

  • R: statnet/ergm for policy networks, RSiena for dynamics, sna + igraph for descriptive work, latentnet for latent space models.
  • GUI: UCINET + NetDraw (the policy-network classics), Gephi for exploration, visone for drawing and analysis together.
  • Qualitative companion: NVivo / ATLAS.ti for interviews and documents that feed the network's story.
  • Benchmark studies & data: Berardo & Scholz's estuary networks, Henry et al.'s ecology networks, Provan & Milward's four-community study — all published with replication material.
